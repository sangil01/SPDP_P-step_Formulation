
## Problem Analysis

### Setting

Let $D(q)$ denote the duration of route $q$, including travel and service times, and let

$$
\bar C(q)=f+c(q)
$$

denote its original cost, where $f>0$ is the fixed cost of using one tractor and $c(q)$ is its variable transport cost. For a route set $\mathcal Q$, define $D(\mathcal Q)=\sum_{q\in\mathcal Q}D(q)$ and $\bar C(\mathcal Q)=\sum_{q\in\mathcal Q}\bar C(q)$.

In the present SPDP, a route starts at its first pickup and ends immediately after its last delivery. Hence the virtual origin and destination satisfy

$$
t_{0v}=c_{0v}=0\quad(v\in C^p),
\qquad
t_{u,2n+1}=c_{u,2n+1}=0\quad(u\in C^d),
\tag{1}
$$

where $t_{uv}$ and $c_{uv}$ denote the travel time and variable transport cost between two action nodes, excluding service time and the vehicle fixed cost. The fixed cost $f$ is charged on each actual origin edge. Equation (1) is essential below.

Moreover, every feasible solution performs one pickup, one emptying, and one delivery per request. Hence its total service duration is the constant $|\mathcal I|(s^p+s^e+s^d)$; only travel duration distinguishes two feasible solutions under the duration objective.

### Effect of splitting at an empty connector

**Proposition 1 (empty-state splitting).** Assume that the available fleet is sufficiently large and its cardinality is not fixed by equality. Consider a feasible route $q$ containing two consecutive action nodes $u\in C^d$ and $v\in C^p$ such that the tractor is empty after serving $u$. Split $q$ at the connector $(u,v)$ into a route $q^L$ ending at $u$ and a route $q^R$ starting at $v$. Then both routes are feasible and

$$
D(q^L)+D(q^R)=D(q)-t_{uv},
\tag{2}
$$

$$
\bar C(q^L)+\bar C(q^R)=\bar C(q)+f-c_{uv}.
\tag{3}
$$

**Proof.** The state after $u$ and the initial state before $v$ are both empty. Replacing the internal connector $u\rightarrow v$ by the free arcs $u\rightarrow(2n+1)$ and $0\rightarrow v$ therefore preserves all action, container-state, and capacity requirements. Since all durations are nonnegative, each child route is no longer than $q$ and hence still satisfies the route-duration limit.

Let $D^-$ be the duration through the service at $u$, let $s_v$ be the service time at $v$, and let $D^+$ be the duration strictly after that service. The service at $v$ is performed once before and after the split, so

$$
D(q)=D^-+t_{uv}+s_v+D^+,
\qquad
D(q^L)+D(q^R)=D^-+s_v+D^+,
$$

which proves (2). Likewise, if $c^-$ and $c^+$ are the variable costs on the two sides of the connector, then

$$
\bar C(q)=f+c^-+c_{uv}+c^+,
$$

whereas the two split routes incur two fixed costs but no origin- or destination-travel cost:

$$
\bar C(q^L)+\bar C(q^R)=2f+c^-+c^+.
$$

This proves (3). $\square$

**Consequently, splitting never increases total duration and decreases it whenever $t_{uv}>0$.** **In contrast, it increases the original cost precisely when $f>c_{uv}$**. In all 48 benchmark files considered here, $f=500$ and the largest entry of any variable-cost distance matrix is 163. **Therefore every relevant empty-state split in this benchmark increases original cost** by at least $500-163=337$.

The reverse operation makes the conflict explicit. Two blocks $b$ and $b'$ can be concatenated state-feasibly because the first ends empty and the second starts empty. If the merged route $b\oplus b'$ satisfies the time limit, then

$$
D(b\oplus b')=D(b)+D(b')+t_{bb'},
\tag{4}
$$

$$
\bar C(b\oplus b')-\bar C(\{b,b'\})=c_{bb'}-f.
\tag{5}
$$

**Hence a merge normally worsens the duration objective but improves the original objective when the saved fixed cost exceeds the connector cost.**

### Atomic blocks

Call a feasible partial path a **block** if it starts with an empty tractor, ends with an empty tractor, satisfies the route-duration limit when attached to the free virtual endpoints, and **contains no internal empty-state delivery-to-pickup connector at which Proposition 1 can be applied.** Repeatedly splitting any feasible route at all such connectors gives its maximal block decomposition.

**Proposition 2 (block dominance for duration minimization).** For every feasible solution $\mathcal Q$, its block decomposition $\mathcal B(\mathcal Q)$ is feasible and satisfies

$$
D\bigl(\mathcal B(\mathcal Q)\bigr)
=D(\mathcal Q)-\sum_{(u,v)\in S(\mathcal Q)}t_{uv}
\le D(\mathcal Q),
\tag{6}
$$

where $S(\mathcal Q)$ is the set of removed empty connectors. Therefore, provided that a sufficiently large fleet is available, **there exists a duration-optimal solution consisting only of blocks.** If every removed connector has positive travel time, every duration-optimal solution is block-separated, apart from equivalent zero-time ties.

**Proof.** If a route contains a splittable connector, apply Proposition 1 and replace that route by its two feasible child routes. This removes at least one internal connector and does not create a new internal connector inside either child. Because a feasible solution contains finitely many action nodes and therefore finitely many internal connectors, the repeated splitting process must terminate after finitely many operations. At termination, no route contains a splittable connector; hence every resulting route is a block. Summing (2) over all removed connectors gives (6).

Now apply this decomposition to a duration-optimal solution $\mathcal Q^*$. The resulting block solution is feasible and has duration at most $D(\mathcal Q^*)$. It cannot have a strictly smaller duration, since that would contradict the optimality of $\mathcal Q^*$. Therefore it has the same optimal value, proving that a block-separated duration-optimal solution exists. $\square$

Let $\mathcal B$ be the set of all feasible blocks, let $a_{ib}=1$ if block $b$ performs action $i\in\bar V=C^p\cup C^d$, and let $d_b=D(b)$. Once $\mathcal B$ is available, Proposition 2 gives the exact block master

$$
\begin{aligned}
\min\quad &\sum_{b\in\mathcal B}d_bz_b\\
\text{s.t.}\quad
&\sum_{b\in\mathcal B}a_{ib}z_b=1
&&\forall i\in\bar V,\\
&z_b\in\{0,1\}
&&\forall b\in\mathcal B.
\end{aligned}
\tag{7}
$$

All sequencing, capacity, emptying, and container-state requirements internal to a block are embedded in its definition. **Equation (7) is still an NP-hard set-partitioning problem, and $|\mathcal B|$ may be exponential**; the result does not make duration minimization polynomial. **Its significance is that connectors between blocks are dominated and need not be considered in a duration-optimal solution**.

### Block merging under the original objective

Two distinct blocks selected in (7) perform disjoint actions, and each block starts and ends with the empty state. Therefore their concatenation is automatically state- and capacity-feasible in either order. The only remaining feasibility condition for merging $b$ followed by $b'$ is the route-duration limit

$$
D(b)+D(b')+t_{bb'}\le T.
\tag{8}
$$

More generally, grouping the selected blocks $b_1,\ldots,b_m$ into one ordered route is feasible if

$$
D(b_1)+\sum_{j=2}^{m}\left(t_{b_{j-1}b_j}+D(b_j)\right)\le T.
\tag{9}
$$

Under duration minimization, every connector time in (9) is nonnegative. Thus operating all selected blocks as separate routes is always at least as good as merging them, which is precisely the dominance established by Proposition 2.

The original objective reverses this incentive. By (5), every time-feasible merge with $c_{bb'}<f$ reduces the original cost by $f-c_{bb'}$. Consequently, selecting the blocks that cover $\bar V$ is no longer sufficient: the model must also decide

1. which selected blocks use the same tractor, and
2. in which order those blocks are performed,

while ensuring (9) for every resulting route. For example, three selected blocks can remain as three routes, be merged into two routes such as $(b_1,b_2)$ and $(b_3)$, or form one of several ordered single routes such as $(b_2,b_1,b_3)$, provided the corresponding duration does not exceed $T$. The order matters because it changes the connector times and costs.

Hence the central distinction is compactly stated as follows: **duration minimization admits a splitting dominance that guarantees an optimum in the block-separated subset, whereas the original objective generally rewards time-feasible block merging and must coordinate block grouping, ordering, and route-duration packing.** Although the two formulations impose the same feasibility constraints, their objective-relevant search spaces differ: duration minimization can be restricted without loss of optimality to the block-separated subset, while the original-cost problem cannot. Duration minimization therefore admits an exact dominance-based reduction of the sufficient search space. This provides a structural reason to expect it to be easier on the present instances, while not proving a lower worst-case complexity or a universal runtime ordering.

Without a fixed vehicle cost, both objectives retain the empty-connector splitting dominance, so merged block sequences can be excluded without loss of optimality. ### Direct analysis

Let $X$ denote the integer feasible set of either the 2-index or the $p$-step formulation, and let $P\supseteq X$ denote its LP relaxation. Write $y$ for the edge-use vector, or for the edge-use vector induced by a $p$-step solution, and define its variable transport cost and number of routes by

$$
C(y)=\sum_{e\in E}\bar c_e y_e,
\qquad
K(y)=\sum_{e\in\delta^+(0):\,v_e\in C^p}y_e,
$$

where $\bar c_e$ excludes the vehicle fixed cost. Since the fixed cost is charged exactly once on each actual depot departure, the objective with fixed cost $f\ge0$ is

$$
F_f(y)=C(y)+fK(y).
\tag{10}
$$

Thus changing $f$ modifies only the objective coefficients; the integer feasible set and its LP relaxation remain unchanged.

**Proposition 3 (monotonicity of the optimal route count).** Let $0\le f_1<f_2$, and let $y^1$ and $y^2$ be optimal solutions of $F_{f_1}$ and $F_{f_2}$, respectively. Then $K(y^2)\le K(y^1)$.

**Proof.** Optimality gives

$$
C(y^1)+f_1K(y^1)\le C(y^2)+f_1K(y^2),
$$

$$
C(y^2)+f_2K(y^2)\le C(y^1)+f_2K(y^1).
$$

Adding the two inequalities yields $(f_2-f_1)(K(y^2)-K(y^1))\le0$, which proves the claim. $\square$

**The variable-cost-only objective has the opposite structural tendency.** By Proposition 1, splitting a feasible route at an empty-state connector $(u,v)$ produces a feasible solution $y'$ satisfying

$$
C(y')=C(y)-c_{uv}\le C(y).
\tag{11}
$$

Hence, when the fleet is sufficiently large, repeated feasible splitting produces a variable-cost-optimal solution in which no positive-cost empty-state connector remains splittable. The operation weakly increases the number of routes but is never penalized when $f=0$; zero-cost connectors may yield tied split and unsplit optima. In contrast,

$$
F_f(y')-F_f(y)=f-c_{uv},
\tag{12}
$$

so a fixed cost exceeding the removed connector cost makes the same split strictly unfavorable. V**ariable-cost minimization and a fixed cost that dominates the relevant connector costs therefore favor, respectively, more separated and more consolidated route structures.**

For an integer route count $k$, define the conditional value functions

$$
\phi_{IP}(k)=\min\{C(y):y\in X,\ K(y)=k\},
\qquad
\phi_{LP}(k)=\min\{C(y):y\in P,\ K(y)=k\}.
\tag{13}
$$

Let

$$
c^*=\min_{y\in X}C(y).
$$

For any feasible integer route count $k$, define

$$
\Delta_{IP}(k)=\phi_{IP}(k)-c^*\ge0.
\tag{14}
$$

**It is strict precisely when no integer solution with $k$ routes attains the global minimum variable cost.** For a given $f\ge0$, let

$$
k^*\in\arg\min_k\{\phi_{IP}(k)+fk\}
$$

be a route count attained by an optimal fixed-cost integer solution. When the fixed cost drives the optimum to the minimum route count, $k^*=k_{\min}$, as in A8 and A9. Suppose more generally that

$$
\phi_{LP}(k^*)\le c^*<\phi_{IP}(k^*),
\tag{15}
$$

so that the LP relaxation admits a fractional solution with aggregate departure flow $k^*$ and variable cost no greater than the globally optimal integer variable cost. Since

$$
z_{IP}(f)=\phi_{IP}(k^*)+fk^*,
$$

$$
z_{LP}(f)\le \phi_{LP}(k^*)+fk^*
\le c^*+fk^*,
$$

and hence the fixed-cost integrality gap satisfies

$$
z_{IP}(f)-z_{LP}(f)
\ge
\phi_{IP}(k^*)-\phi_{LP}(k^*)
\ge \Delta_{IP}(k^*)>0.
\tag{16}
$$

This relation gives rise to a two-stage bound progression. Initially, $\phi_{LP}(k^*)$ may be strictly below $c^*$, keeping the fixed-cost bound below $c^*+fk^*$. Cuts and early branching may eliminate these weakest fractional solutions and raise the effective conditional LP bound to $c^*$. A residual gap nevertheless remains because

$$
c^*<\phi_{IP}(k^*).
$$

Closing this gap requires excluding fractional solutions that violate the valid conditional bound for integer solutions,

$$
y\in X,\quad K(y)=k^*
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
C(y)\ge\phi_{IP}(k^*),
\tag{17}
$$

The aggregate equality $K(y)=k^*$ does not imply (17) in the LP relaxation. For fractional $y$, $K(y)$ measures total departure flow rather than the number of complete routes, and therefore does not ensure a decomposition into $k^*$ integral, state-consistent, duration-feasible paths. The 2-index big-$M$ constraints further weaken duration propagation on fractional edges. The $p$-step formulation strengthens local continuity, but fractional combinations of $p$-steps can still satisfy the aggregate visit, state, and time equations without forming $k^*$ feasible routes. Branching on one edge can therefore leave alternative edge combinations that reproduce a similar fractional structure. Consequently, many descendant-node relaxations may continue to attain or approach

$$
K(y)\approx k^*,
\qquad
C(y)\approx c^*,
$$

causing the global dual bound to stagnate near $c^*+fk^*$.

The difficult instances A8 and A9 illustrate this two-stage bound progression. Here $f=500$ and $k^*=k_{\min}=2$.

| ID  | $c^*$ | $\phi_{IP}(2)$ | $\Delta_{IP}(2)$ | $c^*+2f$ | Fixed-cost optimum | Initial 2-index LP bound | Final 2-index bound | 2-index nodes |
| --- | ----: | -------------: | ---------------: | -------: | -----------------: | -----------------------: | ------------------: | ------------: |
| A8  |   301 |            314 |               13 |     1301 |               1314 |                 1298.110 |                1301 |     7,311,641 |
| A9  |   243 |            244 |                1 |     1243 |               1244 |                 1240.000 |                1243 |    15,441,660 |

In both cases, the initial 2-index bound was below $c^*+2f$ and was subsequently raised to that level, but no further global improvement was obtained after millions of branch-and-bound nodes. The remaining proof obligations were $C(y)\ge314$ and $C(y)\ge244$, respectively, over two-route integer solutions. Thus the observed progression is consistent with the first LP gap being closed while the conditional gap at $k^*=2$ persists. Direct verification requires solving the relaxation with $K(y)=2$ to obtain $\phi_{LP}(2)$; an oracle diagnostic can then compare this restricted model before and after adding $C(y)\ge\phi_{IP}(2)$. The fixed cost does not weaken the formulation itself, but exposes a route-count region in which the relaxation does not readily certify the required conditional variable-cost bound.
