## Two Layers of LP Relaxation Weakness under Fixed Vehicle Costs

### Setting

Let $X$ be the integer feasible set of the SPDP formulation and let $P\supseteq X$ be its LP relaxation. For an edge-use vector $y$, define

$$
C(y)=\sum_{e\in E}\bar c_e y_e,
\qquad
K(y)=\sum_{\substack{e\in E:\,u_e=0\\v_e\in C^p}}y_e,
$$

where $\bar c_e$ excludes the vehicle fixed cost. Since the fixed cost $f$ is charged once on every actual depot departure, the original objective is

$$
F_f(y)=C(y)+fK(y).
\tag{1}
$$

For integer solutions, $K(y)$ is the number of routes. For fractional solutions, however, it is only the aggregate departure flow and need not correspond to a decomposition into $K(y)$ complete, state-consistent, duration-feasible routes. This distinction produces two separate sources of relaxation weakness.

### Layer 1: underestimation of the required route count

Every integer feasible solution satisfies

$$
K(y)\ge k_{\min}.
\tag{2}
$$

The basic LP relaxation need not satisfy (2). It can reduce the fractional flow on the depot-departure edges, distribute flow over internal service-to-service edges, and combine several route structures fractionally while preserving the aggregate visit, state-flow, and time constraints. Consequently, it may admit

$$
K(y)<k_{\min}.
\tag{3}
$$

Under (1), this is directly rewarded by the fixed-cost term. Relative to an integer-feasible route count, a fractional solution with departure deficit $k_{\min}-K(y)$ saves

$$
f\bigl(k_{\min}-K(y)\bigr)
\tag{4}
$$

in fixed cost before accounting for the simultaneous change in $C(y)$. Since $f=500$ in the benchmark instances, even a modest fractional deficit can substantially depress the LP bound.

VI 44 removes precisely this first weakness:

$$
K(y)\ge k_{\min}.
\tag{5}
$$

Hence its large effect on the original-cost LP bound is structural rather than incidental: it excludes the part of the relaxation that obtains an artificial fixed-cost saving by using less departure flow than any feasible integer solution.

### Layer 2: conditional variable-cost gap at the minimum route count

The LP may retain fractional solutions whose total departure flow is $k_{\min}$, but which cannot be decomposed into feasible $k_{\min}$-route integer solutions.

$$
c^*=\min_{y\in X}C(y),
\qquad
\phi_{IP}(k)=\min\{C(y):y\in X,\ K(y)=k\},
$$

and

$$
\phi_{LP}(k)=\min\{C(y):y\in P,\ K(y)=k\}.
\tag{6}
$$

After Layer 1 has been removed, the relaxation may still satisfy

$$
\phi_{LP}(k_{\min})\le c^*
<\phi_{IP}(k_{\min}).
\tag{7}
$$

Thus the LP can attain the globally optimal variable cost, or a still smaller value, while carrying aggregate departure flow $k_{\min}$, even though no $k_{\min}$-route integer solution can attain that cost. The remaining valid implication over integer solutions is

$$
y\in X,\quad K(y)=k_{\min}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
C(y)\ge\phi_{IP}(k_{\min}),
\tag{8}
$$

but (8) is not implied by the single aggregate equality $K(y)=k_{\min}$ in the LP relaxation.

This explains the characteristic branch-and-bound stagnation. Branching on one edge may eliminate one fractional realization of (7), while alternative internal-edge combinations reproduce a similar fractional structure in descendant nodes. Many nodes can therefore continue to return

$$
K(y)\approx k_{\min},
\qquad
C(y)\approx c^*,
\tag{9}
$$

so the global bound remains near $c^*+fk_{\min}$. Branching is exact and must eventually close the gap for a finite binary model, but it can be an inefficient way to approximate the conditional integer hull described by (8).

### Evidence from A8 and A9

For A8 and A9, $f=500$ and $k_{\min}=2$. The exact $p=2$ solutions previously established

$$
\phi_{IP}^{A8}(2)=314,
\qquad
\phi_{IP}^{A9}(2)=244.
\tag{10}
$$

Indeed, the exact original-cost optima are $1314$ and $1244$ and are attained with two routes. Subtracting $2f=1000$ gives the feasible conditional values in (10); any two-route solution with a smaller variable cost would produce a smaller original-cost objective and contradict the previously proved optima.

In the unrestricted travel-cost-only problem, the optima are

$$
c_{A8}^*=301,
\qquad
c_{A9}^*=243,
\tag{11}
$$

and both are solved at the root because integer solutions with those costs are available when additional routes are not penalized. In contrast, the fixed-$K=2$ travel-cost models produced the following results.

| ID | Root LP before cuts | Final lower bound | Incumbent | Proven conditional optimum | Nodes in 1,200 s |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| A8 | 298.1096 | 301 | 314 | 314 | 4,927,974 |
| A9 | 240 | 243 | 244 | 244 | 10,230,681 |

In both instances, generic root cuts first raise the relaxation to $c^*$, thereby closing the weaker part of the LP gap. The bound then remains at $c^*$ despite millions of nodes. The unresolved proof obligations are exactly

$$
C(y)\ge314\quad\text{when }K(y)=2
$$

for A8 and

$$
C(y)\ge244\quad\text{when }K(y)=2
$$

for A9. These results isolate Layer 2: even after the route count is fixed at its correct integer value, the relaxation does not represent the corresponding conditional variable-cost bound.

### Why duration minimization is comparatively easy

Let $D(y)$ be total route duration. The duration-only objective is

$$
\min D(y),
\tag{12}
$$

and contains no term $fK(y)$. Therefore, a fractional reduction of $K(y)$ below $k_{\min}$ creates no direct objective saving analogous to (4). Layer 1 may still describe a relaxation artifact in the feasible region, but it is not amplified by a large objective coefficient, and the solver is not required to optimize the route count.

More importantly, duration minimization does not require a certificate of the form (8) at $k_{\min}$. The objective does not drive the solution toward the minimum number of routes. On the contrary, splitting a feasible route at an empty-state connector $(u,v)$ preserves feasibility and satisfies

$$
D(y^{\mathrm{split}})=D(y)-t_{uv}\le D(y).
\tag{13}
$$

Thus, when the fleet is sufficiently large, a duration-optimal solution exists in the block-separated subset, and increasing the number of routes through feasible splitting is not penalized. The difficult conflict underlying the original objective—simultaneously attaining a small route count and the best possible variable cost—does not arise.

The contrast can therefore be summarized as follows:

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\text{Layer 1: }&K(y)<k_{\min}
&&\text{artificially saves }fK(y);\ \text{VI 44 removes it},\\
\text{Layer 2: }&K(y)=k_{\min},\ C(y)<\phi_{IP}(k_{\min})
&&\text{remains after VI 44 and causes bound stagnation},\\
\text{Duration: }&\min D(y)
&&\text{contains neither the fixed-cost reward nor the conditional }k_{\min}\text{ proof obligation}.
\end{aligned}}
\tag{14}
$$

This does not imply that duration minimization is polynomial or that its LP relaxation is always integral. It identifies why the two fixed-cost-driven mechanisms observed in the original-cost formulation are inactive under the duration objective, providing a structural explanation for the strong empirical performance of the basic duration formulation on the present instances.
